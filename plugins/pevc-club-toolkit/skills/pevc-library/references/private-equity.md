## Why private markets grew

The context *Private Equity 101* opens with: US listed companies fell from roughly 8,000 at the peak to about 3,000 in 2016, recovering to around 6,000; over the same period private equity AUM reached an all-time high of **$9.8 trillion in 2021** (McKinsey, 2022). Companies stay private longer — easier private fundraising, lighter regulatory burden, lower cost, less loss of control. More of the growth happens before anyone can buy it on an exchange.

## How PE differs from VC

Same basic idea — buy ownership in private companies — but the mechanics diverge:

| | Venture capital | Buyout private equity |
|---|---|---|
| Stake | Minority | Usually control |
| Company | Young, often unprofitable | Mature, cash-generative |
| Leverage | Essentially none | Central to the model |
| Return driver | A few outliers (power law) | Operational improvement, deleveraging, multiple expansion |
| Failure tolerance | Most investments fail | Most are expected to work |

Both run the same fund shape: committed LP capital, GP management, management fee plus carry, a fixed life, a J-curve.

Stages across the private spectrum: venture → growth equity → buyout → distressed / special situations, with mezzanine and private credit alongside.

## The buy-out, in plain terms

A leveraged buy-out acquires a company using a mix of equity and borrowed money, with the target's own cash flows servicing the debt. Returns come from three places:

1. **Deleveraging** — cash flow pays down debt, so the equity's share of enterprise value grows.
2. **Operational improvement** — revenue growth and margin expansion raise EBITDA.
3. **Multiple expansion** — exiting at a higher multiple than entry. The least reliable, and the one investors are least entitled to count on.

The INSEAD material in the VC shelf (*Value Creation 2.0*) makes the modern argument that the first and third have thinned out and operational improvement now has to carry the return.

## Management buy-outs

The BDO guide is written for managers buying their own company, and it is useful because it sets out the sequence rather than the theory:

Spot the opportunity → decide why a buy-out → assemble the management team → approach the owner and get consent → appoint financial advisers → prepare the business plan → identify sources of finance → take tax advice → review funding offers → negotiate with the owners → heads of agreement → due diligence → negotiate legal documentation → complete, and then run the company after.

It reads the deal from three sides, which is the part worth teaching: the **venture capitalist's** perspective (return required, structure, control), the **lender's** perspective (cash flow cover, security, covenants), and the **management team's** perspective (what they invest, what they get, what they risk personally). The guide also works a leveraged return through in practice, and covers how to choose between competing backers and banks.

## What the research actually finds

Ljungqvist's CFA Institute review is the serious academic survey in the shelf. It covers: a brief history of PE, key institutional features, measured performance, and — the most contested part — **performance attribution and value creation**. Use it as the counterweight whenever a pitch quotes industry-average returns: the literature argues about benchmark choice, self-reported valuations, the difference between IRR and multiple-based measures, and how much of the return is genuine operational value creation rather than leverage or timing.

If a student cites a PE return figure in a thesis, the first question is which measure, against which benchmark, and net or gross.

## Adjacent material on the shelf

- **M&A** — deal rationale, process, synergies and why most acquisitions underperform their promise.
- **Project finance** — financing an asset on its own ring-fenced cash flows rather than a sponsor's balance sheet. Relevant for infrastructure, energy and anything capital-intensive.
- **Valuing firms in distress** (Damodaran) — the warning that standard DCF and comparables both assume a going concern. Terminal value assumes infinite life; relative valuation prices the company like healthy peers. When survival is genuinely in doubt, both overstate value, and the fix is to model the probability of distress explicitly.
- **Financial modelling fundamentals** (BPM / Spreadsheet Standards Review Board) — modelling discipline: separate assumptions from calculations from outputs, build the operational, working capital, tax, asset, capital and financial-statement blocks as distinct sections, and include checks. Hardcoded numbers destroy the ability to test anything; circular references are a defect, not a feature.

## Honest limits

This shelf is thinner and more general than the VC shelf. It covers fund mechanics, the buy-out playbook and the supporting technical skills well. It does *not* contain a worked LBO model, PE-specific diligence templates, or current deal data. When a student needs any of those, say so and search, rather than stretching the material.
