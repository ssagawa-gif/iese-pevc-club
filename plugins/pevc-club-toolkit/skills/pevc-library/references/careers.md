## The interview question bank

Three blocks. Prepare written answers to all of them; the point is not to memorise but to find out where your thinking is thin.

**About you**
- Why venture capital, and why now?
- Where do you want to be in five years?
- Biggest strengths and most significant challenges?
- Why VC rather than joining a startup or founding one?
- What were you responsible for in your last role? What did you like and not like?
- Tell us something that isn't on your resume.

**The VC and startup market**
- What are the key trends in venture right now — investing activity, exits, VC fundraising?
- What is your favourite startup right now? Be ready with: description, problem, solution, why it matters, founders, and **fit with this firm** (stage, geography, sector).
- Which sectors are attractive to you?
- What company or market do you think is overvalued?
- The most interesting exit of the year?
- What are your top questions in a first meeting with a founder?
- What VC resources do you read?

**The firm and the role**
- Why this firm? How are we different from our competitors?
- Which portfolio company are you most excited about?
- **Which portfolio company would you have passed on?**
- What do you expect your day to day to look like?
- How would you run diligence on a company you like?
- How would you source companies?

Plus your own questions for them — have three, and make them specific to the firm.

## How to prepare, in order of payoff

1. **Have a live market view.** The trends question is the one that separates candidates. It needs current numbers — investing activity, exit environment, fundraising — not last year's. Look them up the week of the interview and say the date.
2. **Build one deep company take.** One startup you can discuss for ten minutes: what it does, the problem, why now, the founders, the risks you see, and why it fits *this* firm's thesis. Depth on one beats shallow coverage of five.
3. **Prepare the pass.** "Which portfolio company would you have passed on" tests whether you can disagree with your interviewer respectfully and with evidence. Pick one, state the thesis you would have been sceptical of, name what would have changed your mind, and be gracious about it.
4. **Know their thesis cold.** Read the firm's own writing — their published theses, their recent investments, who led them. Walking in without this reads as not caring.
5. **Have a sourcing answer that is actually yours.** The sourcing data (see `sourcing-and-diligence.md`) shows ~32% of deals come from former colleagues and ~30% from outbound. Your answer should describe a network and a method you really have, not a generic "I'd use PitchBook".

## What the other side of the table is asking

If you want to practise thinking like the investor, the 15 questions VCs ask founders before investing (see `term-sheets-and-memos.md`) are the mirror image of the interview: great team, big market, early traction, passionate founders, founders who know their own numbers, warm referral, professional deck, risks and mitigations, product quality, use of funds, realistic valuation, differentiated technology, IP, realistic projections, clean legal formation.

A useful exercise: take a company you admire, answer all fifteen as if you were its founder, then answer them again as the investor deciding.

## Angel investing

The shelf includes an introduction to angel investing, which is the most accessible way for a student to get real reps. The honest framing from the library: angels target 5–10x cash-on-cash over 4–8 years and must account for 3–5x divergence from dilution (see `valuation.md`), they want 1–10% ownership and usually no control, and the failure rate is high enough that one investment is not a portfolio.

## Reality check

From the library's own numbers: investors see 300–500 deals for every one they fund, and most form a view in the first 10–15 minutes. The same pattern recognition that makes an experienced investor fast also encodes bias — HBS research in the material documented male and female founders being asked systematically different questions. Worth knowing from both sides of the table.
