# Sourcing and due diligence

Modules 2 and 3 of the club library. Sources: *Deal Sourcing, Analysis and Due Diligence* (Les Alexander, Darden Venture Capital Learning Series, which cites Harvard Business Review's *How Venture Capitalists Make Decisions*), *Venture Capital Due Diligence* (Robert Carroll, Oasis500), *GCV Investor Due Diligence Checklist*, *Strategic Deal Sourcing for Family Offices*.

## The investment process end to end

Source deals → evaluate → structure and negotiate → close → monitor → exit.

Sourcing and closing are usually done by senior partners; the analysis underneath is done by associates and analysts with partner oversight.

## Where deals actually come from

Harvard Business Review's survey, as reported in the Darden deck:

| Channel | Share |
|---|---|
| The VC's former colleagues | 32% |
| Initiating contact with entrepreneurs (outbound) | 30% |
| Referrals from other investors | 20% |
| Cold emails from entrepreneurs | 10% |
| Referrals from portfolio companies | 8% |

**Inbound** sourcing rests on a maintained network: accelerators, angels, current and former portfolio executives, other VC firms, lawyers, accountants, bankers. **Outbound** sourcing means picking attractive industries and approaching targets directly — often before they are formally raising, which tends to mean less competition and better terms.

A written investment thesis is itself a sourcing tool: it defines stage, sector, geography and cheque size, which keeps the pipeline focused and makes screening fast.

## The funnel, with real numbers

One firm's annual shape: 300 deals received → 100 pass the thesis screen → 20–25 teams met → 10–15 researched in depth → 6 term sheets signed → 3–5 closed.

HBR's per-deal version: for every 101 investments reviewed, ~28 reach a management meeting, ~10 a partner meeting, ~4.8 diligence, ~1.7 a term sheet, 1 closes. Average deal takes 83 days to close, 118 hours of due diligence and around 10 reference calls.

## What investors say drives the decision

| Factor | Said it was important |
|---|---|
| Quality of the founding team | 95% |
| Business model | 74% |
| Market | 68% |
| Industry | 31% |
| Valuation | under 30% |

And: 20% of all VCs — 31% of early-stage VCs — do not forecast company financials at all before investing. Asked what drives returns, they name deal *selection* well ahead of sourcing or post-investment help.

## Three stages of diligence

**1. Screening.** Does it fit the mandate — stage, geography, cheque size, sector? Some funds only look at referred opportunities.

**2. Business diligence.**
- *Team* — background, domain expertise, role, and time actually spent with them. The MacMillan criteria used in the library: sustained intense effort, reacts well to risk, articulate about the venture, attends to detail, familiar with the market, demonstrated leadership, referred by someone trustworthy.
- *Business model* — walk the Business Model Canvas: key partners, activities, resources, value proposition, customer relationships, channels, segments, cost structure, revenue streams.
- *Go to market* — can they state the value proposition simply, explain how they reach customers, and describe the competition accurately?
- *The angle* — **what insight has the founding team made that the market hasn't realised yet?** The single best diligence question in the library.
- *Market size* — do your own research; test their assumptions rather than accepting them. What are incumbents doing? How could a startup disrupt this?
- *References* — people who know the founders, people who know the industry, and actual customers.
- *Financing plan* — are the projections' assumptions sound? Is the raise enough to reach the next milestone (growth stage: 12–18 months)?

**3. Legal diligence.** A lawyer reads everything. What you are looking for: fired co-founders, undisclosed debt, consultants owed shares, pending litigation — and how the founder behaves while negotiating.

Diligence changes with stage: a startup offers an idea and projections, a later-stage company offers a track record and customer data.

## Risk: identify → assess → mitigate → manage

Sources of risk to work through explicitly: **company** (execution, missed milestones, margins, cash-out, operations that will not scale, fraud) · **industry** (declining fundamentals, input prices, cyclicality, bad press) · **competition** (dominant incumbents, price wars, low barriers, foreign entrants) · **customer** (fads, demographics, service failures) · **supplier** (supply chain, shortages, delivery, contracts) · **management** (wrong leader, cannot scale, key-person risk, misrepresentation) · **employee** (thin labour market, costs, retention) · **technology** (obsolescence, failure to commercialise, IP) · **regulatory** (new rules, lost tax credits, tariffs) · **geography** (natural disaster, FX, political stability) · **economic** (recession, inflation).

Mitigations, matched: references and background checks for management; research and consultants for industry; customer calls and surveys for demand; budget testing and downside modelling for financials.

**Investor-side risks** the same deck flags: FOMO, chasing or falling in love with a deal, accepting "too high" valuations, unreasonably short decision deadlines, unwillingness to share information, inconsistent information, and ignoring your own gut.

The decision rule is simple: every deal carries risk; the question is whether you can manage it. If you cannot, walk.

## Investment committee

Partners meet regularly — often weekly — to review and approve. Common two-stage shape: initial evaluation and draft term sheet → **IC stage 1** → term sheet negotiated and signed, diligence and documentation completed → **IC stage 2** → close.
