# Portfolio management, value creation and exits

Modules 7 and 8 of the club library. Sources: *Fund Operations: Portfolio Management & Construction*, *Value Creation 2.0* (INSEAD), *The Path to Liquidity in Venture Capital* (Ana Khoo, AVP, using Cambridge Associates data), *Stages of VC Financing*.

## Two portfolio philosophies

**Conviction model** — a small, concentrated portfolio (the library cites 10–15 companies in today's market). The fund usually **leads** rounds, takes meaningful ownership, reserves capital to defend its percentage in follow-ons, and is hands-on. The constraint is time: each company takes real partner attention, which caps how many you can hold. The risk is a later investor arriving and buying most of a round you wanted.

**Diversification model** — a large portfolio (30+, and in practice 50–100). The fund rarely leads, so ownership per company is lower, but capital is preserved for more companies and more follow-ons. GPs usually do not take board seats; most of their time goes to sourcing and vetting. The risks: hard follow-on decisions, and fund-size creep — too many small cheques means too little ownership anywhere to hit the return target.

**Hybrids.** A *venture studio* is a conviction strategy that builds companies internally and spins them out with high ownership. *Accelerators* can run either way — many small bets, or a small hands-on cohort — and are sometimes used purely as deal flow for a larger fund.

## Portfolio construction arithmetic

From the library's example breakdown: a fund may hold 45–50 investments; only 1 or 2 will return the entire fund; only 6–8 will produce a fund-level return (25%+ of fund cost).

Two consequences the source draws out:
- Lead early and take real risk on market size, timing and product. Companies that end up successful often do not look successful early.
- Fund culture has to make risk safe to take, and performance has to be judged at fund level, not partner by partner.

**Reinvestment rule of thumb:** follow on when post-investment data is exciting *and* the company looks able to raise downstream capital.

## Monitoring

- **Board representation** — most VCs take 1, sometimes 2 seats. Board members owe a fiduciary duty to all stakeholders, not just their fund.
- **Information rights** — interim financials, annual budgets and forecasts, audited statements.
- **Informal contact** — regular calls and visits; the deal team owns the relationship.
- **Weekly partner meetings** — each partner walks their companies.
- **Portfolio reviews** — quarterly, semi-annual or annual deep reviews to assess risk and set future allocation.

**What to look at, by stage:**

| Stage | Financial focus | Business focus |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-revenue | Operating expenses, cash burn | Product development, team additions |
| Early revenue | Opex, burn, revenue | Product, customer feedback, team, sales pipeline |
| Near / at profitability | Revenue, gross margin, opex, cash | Pipeline, bookings, team, marketing plan |

The underlying questions are always about cash: how much is made, how fast it is lost, and when the company expects to stop losing it. Focus on trend, not variance — early-stage numbers move, and the point is understanding *what changed and what has to change*.

Decision-making itself is described in the library as pattern matching built from volume: "you look at a lot of art… at some point something clicks". Useful, and also exactly where bias enters — treat pattern recognition as a hypothesis generator, not a verdict.

## Value creation after the cheque

The five things funds actually do: **financing and exit transactions** (introductions to lead investors, debt relationships, IPO process), **strategic advice**, **team building and recruiting** (most firms are active in hiring senior management), **leveraging contacts** (contract negotiation, business development, sales, sourcing the exit), and **crisis management**. Funds that negotiate better deal structure usually justify it with this value-add.

INSEAD's *Value Creation 2.0* covers the same ground on the PE side, where operational improvement rather than multiple expansion is the stated source of returns.

## Exits and the path to liquidity

Routes: **M&A** (the common one), **IPO**, **secondary sale**, and in the worst case liquidation.

**How long it actually takes.** AVP analysed US fund vintages 2000–2015 using Cambridge Associates data, measuring years to reach 1.0x DPI — the point where a fund has returned investors' cost *in cash*:

- Upper-quartile funds: at least **8 years** (worst vintage, 2000: 13 years).
- Median funds: at least **9.5 years** (worst vintage, 2002: 19 years).
- The gap between median and upper quartile has narrowed — ~6 extra years for 2001–04 vintages, ~2 extra years for 2010–14.

A pre-seed manager investing pre-product will always take longer than a Series B manager investing into traction, regardless of quality.

## Secondaries

Liquidity pressure after 2022 — higher rates, a closed IPO window, few distributions — pushed both LPs and GPs toward the secondary market.

- VC fund secondaries volume rose **151.3%** in H1 2024 ($2.7bn vs $1.1bn in H1 2023), partly on one $1bn portfolio purchase by Lexington Partners.
- Dedicated funds grew: StepStone closed the largest VC-secondaries fund at $3.3bn; Industry Ventures closed its tenth at $1.45bn; broader-mandate vehicles from Lexington ($22.7bn) and Hamilton Lane ($5.6bn) also grew sharply.
- **LP-led** deals are portfolio sales for liquidity. **GP-led** deals — especially **continuation funds** — move assets into a new vehicle with reset terms, letting LPs sell, hold or re-invest and letting GPs hold winners longer.
- Pricing moved from the high-70s/low-80s percent of NAV to the high-60s/low-70s after the correction.

**Expected returns on secondary deals, H1 2024 (AVP):**

| | Venture | Private equity |
|---|---|---|
| Expected IRR | ~20–25% | ~15–20% |
| Expected multiple | ~2.0–2.5x | ~1.5–2.0x |
| Expected discount | ~29–45% (55–71% of NAV) | ~6–10% (90–94% of NAV) |

VC secondaries price at wider discounts because the underlying assets are riskier and pricing information is scarcer.

The conclusion the paper draws, worth quoting to any student who thinks of VC as fast money: returning your cost takes a long time, in every quartile. Venture is a game of patience.
