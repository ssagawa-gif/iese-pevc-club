# Memo craft and evidence standards

For Blocks 9, 10, 13, 14 and the final document.
Distilled from the club library's memo templates and real investment memos.

## What a real investment memo looks like

The library holds Bessemer's 2009 Twilio seed memo. Four things it does that a student memo usually does not:

1. **Recommends plainly in the first paragraph.** "We recommend that BVP participate… in a $125,000 extension." The ask, the amount and the structure come before the argument.
2. **States weak numbers in full.** "Growth has not been explosive (they are currently doing about $20k of MRR 9 months after launch)." It does not bury the bad figure.
3. **Separates what is known from what is being watched.** It names three unconverted logos as *leading indicators*, then says explicitly it has not verified whether they are real commitments or trials.
4. **Names its own gaps.** "We haven't dug deep enough into the market dynamics to get comfort around what percentage of the pie will be addressable." A memo that admits the hole is more credible than one that papers over it.

It also carries concrete unit economics — cost per minute of 0.6 cents against ~3 cents charged, 56% blended gross margin — rather than adjectives.

## The YC memo skeleton

Useful as a checklist for the final document:

Introduction (a paragraph each): what do you do · what problem · how the world works now · how you solve it · how solving it changes behavior and makes money · scale of the opportunity. Then: traction and metrics (with a chart) · challenges to growth · market · **future states** (what happens to the market as you win, and where that leads) · competitive landscape · team · **FAQ** (surface the main objections and knock them down — the part where a memo beats a deck) · use of funds.

The two sections students skip are the two that matter most: *future states* is Block 4 done properly, and the *FAQ* is Block 10.

## Sequoia's deck order, for comparison

Company purpose (one declarative sentence) · problem · solution · **why now** · market size · competition · product · business model · team · financials. "Why now" sits fourth, before market size, because the size only matters if the timing argument holds.

## How investors actually decide — use this to calibrate Blocks 7, 9 and 10

From the Harvard Business Review research cited in the library's Darden deck:

- **What VCs say matters:** founding team 95%, business model 74%, market 68%, industry 31%, valuation under 30%.
- **The funnel:** of ~101 investments reviewed, ~28 reach a management meeting, ~10 a partner meeting, ~4.8 diligence, ~1.7 a term sheet, 1 closes. Another firm's version: 300 deals in, 100 pass the thesis screen, 20–25 meetings, 6 term sheets, 3–5 closed.
- **Effort per deal:** ~83 days to close, ~118 hours of diligence, ~10 reference calls.
- **Where deals come from:** former colleagues 32%, outbound to entrepreneurs 30%, referrals from other investors 20%, portfolio referrals 8%, cold email 10%.
- 20% of all VCs and 31% of early-stage VCs do **not** forecast company financials before investing — deal selection, not the model, is what they credit for returns.

The implication for a thesis: an argument that rests mostly on a spreadsheet is arguing in the wrong currency. The team, the market and the model carry the weight.

## The angle

The library's diligence material (Tunguz, via the Darden deck) reduces the whole exercise to one question: **what insight has the founding team made that the market hasn't yet realized?** That is Block 9 in one sentence. If there is no answer, there is no thesis.

## Risk taxonomy for Block 10

Work the list rather than writing "execution risk":

- **Company** — execution, missed milestones, margins, running out of funding, operations that do not scale.
- **Industry** — declining fundamentals, input prices, cyclicality, hostile press.
- **Competition** — dominant incumbents, price cutting, low barriers, foreign entrants.
- **Customer** — shifting attitudes, demographics, service failure.
- **Supplier** — supply chain, shortages, single-source dependency.
- **Management** — the wrong leader, inability to scale with the company, key-person risk.
- **Technology** — obsolescence, failure to commercialise, IP disputes.
- **Regulatory** — new rules, removal of subsidies or tax credits, tariffs.
- **Geography / economic** — political stability, FX, recession, inflation.

And the behavioural risks the same source flags for the investor: FOMO, falling in love with a deal, accepting "too high" valuations, unreasonably short decision deadlines, inconsistent information, and ignoring your own gut.

## The three stages of diligence

**Screening** (does it fit the mandate: stage, geography, cheque size, sector) → **business** (team, business model, go-to-market, the angle, market size verified independently, references, customers, roadmap, financing plan) → **legal**.

For a thesis, the useful import is stage two: *do your own research on the market size — are their assumptions sound?* The same standard applies to the student's own thesis.

## Kill criteria

Kill criteria are the memo's FAQ turned into dated triggers. A good one is observable from outside the company, carries a date, and would actually change behaviour. "If regulation X has not passed by Q4 2027, the unlock never arrives and I stop." A criterion the author would rationalise away is not a criterion.
